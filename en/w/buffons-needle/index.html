Buffon’s needle

Math & Computing

Category: Math & Computing数 理情 報

In short

Drop needles onto a floor ruled with evenly spaced parallel lines, work out the chance a needle crosses a line, and pi appears.

The story

The 18th-century French naturalist the Comte de Buffon devised it. If the needle is as long as the gap between lines, the chance it crosses one is 2 divided by pi. So drop enough needles and count the crossings, and you can estimate pi. It’s uncanny that pi shows up in an experiment that seems to have nothing to do with circles.

The lens

In 1901 the Italian mathematician Mario Lazzarini reported dropping a needle 3,408 times and getting pi right to six decimal places. The result is so good that he’s suspected of stopping at a convenient moment. It teaches both the fun of computing with randomness and the trickiness of handling results.

Drop it casually

“I spilled toothpicks on the floor and now I want to estimate pi with Buffon’s needle.”
