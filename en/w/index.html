History

  1. DefenestrationThe act of throwing someone out of a window. Yes, there is a word just for that.
  2. Year Without a Summer1816, when much of the Northern Hemisphere never got a real summer, most likely because of a giant volcanic eruption the year before.
  3. South Sea BubbleA spectacular boom and crash of shares in Britain in 1720, and the classic example of a speculative bubble.
  4. LysenkoismThe distortion of science when politically desired conclusions are put ahead of evidence.
  5. Gordian knotA problem no one can untangle, and by extension, solving it with one bold stroke that ignores the usual rules.
  6. Banana republicA mocking term for an unstable country whose economy and politics are controlled by a few foreign companies and a small elite.
  7. Potemkin villageA show of prosperity built only to hide how poor things really are.
  8. Pyrrhic victoryA win that costs so much it’s practically a defeat.
  9. Ems DispatchThe 1870 episode in which the Prussian chancellor Bismarck trimmed a telegram from his king, published it, and provoked war with France.
  10. War of Jenkins’ EarA war between Britain and Spain that began in 1739. One pretext for it was a sea captain’s severed ear.
  11. Football WarA short war between Honduras and El Salvador in 1969, sparked by World Cup qualifying matches.
  12. Pig WarA military standoff in 1859 on islands where the US–British border hadn’t been settled, triggered by the shooting of a single pig.
  13. Emu WarThe 1932 episode in Western Australia when the army tried to cull crop-raiding emus with machine guns, and it didn’t go well.
  14. Anglo-Zanzibar WarThe 1896 war between Britain and Zanzibar, said to be the shortest war on record.
  15. Dunkirk spiritA British expression for the resolve to pull together and get through a desperate situation.
  16. Mary CelesteA sailing ship found drifting in the Atlantic in 1872 with not a single person aboard.
  17. Roanoke ColonyA late-16th-century English colony in North America whose settlers vanished without a trace. The Lost Colony.
  18. Dancing plague of 1518An episode in 1518 in Strasbourg, now in France, when crowds of people reportedly danced for days on end.
  19. Westphalian systemThe modern idea of international order in which sovereign states stand as equals and don’t interfere in one another’s internal affairs.
  20. The Great GameThe 19th-century rivalry between Britain and Russia for dominance in Central Asia, played out through spies, explorers, and intrigue.
  21. Treaty of TordesillasThe 1494 treaty in which Spain and Portugal drew a single line down the Atlantic and divided the world beyond Europe between them.
  22. Neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empireVoltaire’s quip about the Holy Roman Empire, now used for anything whose name has nothing to do with reality.
  23. Dark AgesA label for the European Middle Ages after the fall of Rome as a time of cultural decline. Historians rarely use it today.
  24. Whig historyA way of telling history as a straight march of progress toward the present, judging people of the past by whether they helped get us here.
  25. Longue duréeAn approach to history that looks not at events and individuals but at structures, like geography, climate, and economics, that change slowly over centuries.
  26. Columbian exchangeThe vast transfer of crops, livestock, diseases, and people between the Americas and Europe, Asia, and Africa after 1492.
  27. El DoradoA legendary land of gold thought to lie deep in South America. It originally meant “the gilded man.”
  28. Prester JohnA legendary king that medieval Europeans believed ruled a great Christian kingdom somewhere in the East.
  29. Liberum vetoA rule in early modern Poland under which a single legislator’s objection could nullify everything the parliament had decided.
  30. Sakoku-ronThe word sakoku, “closed country,” used for Japan’s Edo-period foreign policy, was actually a translation coined late in that era.
  31. FlagellantsGroups of believers in medieval Europe who marched from town to town whipping themselves.
  32. VasaA lavish Swedish warship that sank in 1628 barely a kilometer and a half into her maiden voyage.
  33. ThermidorThe 1794 coup in the French Revolution that toppled Robespierre, architect of the Terror. By extension, the backlash after a revolution goes too far.
  34. 18 BrumaireThe day in 1799 when Napoleon seized power in a coup. Also known from the title of a work by Marx.
  35. The ten lost daysWhen the Gregorian calendar was adopted in 1582, October 4 was followed by October 15, erasing ten days.
  36. French Republican calendarThe calendar created during the French Revolution, with ten-day weeks and months named after the natural world of each season.
  37. Great Molasses FloodThe 1919 disaster in Boston when a giant storage tank burst and a wave of molasses swept through the streets.
  38. Herostratic fameFame achieved through a misdeed by someone determined to be known at any cost.
  39. Damnatio memoriaeIn ancient Rome, chiseling the names and faces of people to be erased from memory off inscriptions and statues. “Condemnation of memory.”

Society

  1. Goodhart’s lawWhen a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
  2. Streisand effectTrying to hide or remove a piece of information only makes it spread further.
  3. Conspicuous consumptionSpending not because you need the thing, but to show off your wealth or status.
  4. Jevons paradoxMaking the use of a resource more efficient can end up increasing how much of it we consume.
  5. Thomas theorem“If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.”
  6. Parkinson’s law of trivialityOrganizations rush through big, difficult decisions and spend ages arguing over trivial ones everyone can weigh in on.
  7. Lindy effectFor things that don’t wear out, like books and ideas, the longer they have already survived, the longer they are likely to last.
  8. PotlatchA feast held by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest coast, where the host shows status by giving away wealth, and sometimes by destroying it.
  9. Cobra effectWhen a reward meant to solve a problem ends up making the problem worse.
  10. Peltzman effectWhen safety measures make people feel secure, they may take more risks, canceling out some of the benefit.
  11. AnomieA state in which a society’s norms have loosened so much that people no longer know what to aim for or what rules to follow.
  12. Tragedy of the commonsThe paradox that when everyone acts rationally for themselves, a shared resource gets used up.
  13. Overton windowThe range of ideas a society considers acceptable to discuss seriously. That window shifts over time.
  14. Matthew effectThe phenomenon in which those already at an advantage gain still more advantage. The rich get richer.
  15. Hawthorne effectPeople behave differently when they know they’re being watched.
  16. Dilbert principleThe cynical rule that the least competent workers get promoted into management, where they can do the least damage.
  17. Bullshit jobsPointless jobs that even the people doing them secretly believe the world would be fine without.
  18. Shadow workUnpaid, invisible work that someone has to do to keep society and the economy running.
  19. Emotional laborManaging your own feelings as part of your job, producing the right expressions and attitude for customers.
  20. Cultural capital“Capital” that isn’t money but is passed down as education, ways of speaking, tastes, and credentials, and that brings social advantages.
  21. The strength of weak tiesThe idea that casual acquaintances, more than close friends, often bring us new information and opportunities.
  22. Six degrees of separationThe idea that anyone in the world can be reached through a chain of about six acquaintances.
  23. Moral panicWhen a group or trend is played up far beyond reality as a threat to social order and values.
  24. Imagined communitiesThe idea that a nation is a community held together by huge numbers of people who will never meet, imagining that they belong together.
  25. Invented traditionWhen a tradition that seems ancient was actually created fairly recently.
  26. OrientalismThe Western habit of portraying “the East” as mysterious, backward, and fundamentally other, which helped justify domination.
  27. Hare and keA concept from Japanese folklore studies that divides time into hare, the extraordinary days of festivals and rituals, and ke, ordinary everyday life.
  28. MarebitoIn Japanese folk belief, a god who visits at set times from another world, such as across the sea, bringing blessings.
  29. BricolageMaking what you need by combining whatever materials and tools happen to be on hand.
  30. LiminalityThe in-between state of having left an old position without yet having entered a new one.
  31. Thick descriptionDescribing human behavior not just by its surface movements but along with its context and meaning.
  32. NaciremaAn anthropologist’s description of the bizarre rituals of a “primitive tribe” that was really a satire of everyday American life.
  33. Kula ringA ceremonial exchange in the South Pacific in which shell necklaces and armbands are passed from island to island in fixed directions, forever.
  34. The original affluent societyThe idea that hunter-gatherer life was not poor and harsh but an “affluent society” in which modest wants were easily met.
  35. Law of JanteThe unspoken Scandinavian code that says don’t think you’re special and don’t think you’re better than anyone else.
  36. Bowling AloneA phrase for the decline in participation in local clubs and groups, and the thinning of ties between people.
  37. Homo Ludens“Man the player”: the idea that play is what gave rise to human culture.
  38. DramaturgyAnalyzing social life as a stage on which people perform the role of themselves.
  39. Total institutionAn institution, like a prison, an army, or a boarding school, where every part of life is run under one authority.
  40. Labeling theoryThe idea that “deviance” isn’t a property of an act itself but arises when others stick the label “deviant” on a person.
  41. McDonaldizationThe spread of the fast-food restaurant’s principles, efficiency, calculability, predictability, and control, into every area of society.
  42. Post-truthA situation in which appeals to emotion and personal belief shape public opinion more than objective facts do.
  43. Filter bubbleBeing surrounded only by information that matches your tastes and views because of how online search and recommendation systems work.

Psychology

  1. Baader–Meinhof phenomenonThe moment you learn a new word or idea, you suddenly start seeing it everywhere. Also called the frequency illusion.
  2. Diderot effectGetting one new thing makes everything around it look shabby, so you end up replacing the lot.
  3. Zeigarnik effectWe tend to remember unfinished or interrupted tasks better than completed ones.
  4. Ben Franklin effectWe tend to like people we have done a favor for more than people who have done one for us.
  5. PareidoliaSeeing faces or meaningful shapes where there are none: in clouds, stains on a wall, or the holes of an electrical outlet.
  6. Hedonic treadmillAfter good or bad events, people’s happiness tends to drift back to where it was.
  7. IKEA effectThe tendency to value things we’ve put effort into making more highly than they deserve.
  8. AnchoringWhen the first number or piece of information you see becomes an anchor that drags your later judgments toward it.
  9. Bystander effectThe more people are present, the less likely anyone is to step in and help.
  10. Normalcy biasThe tendency, even when facing an emergency, to think “it’s nothing serious” or “we’re still fine.”
  11. Spotlight effectThe tendency to believe that your appearance and slip-ups get much more attention from others than they really do.
  12. Curse of knowledgeOnce you know something, it becomes hard to imagine what it’s like not to know it.
  13. Planning fallacyThe tendency to underestimate how much time and money a task will take, despite past experience.
  14. Endowment effectThe tendency to value something more highly simply because you own it.
  15. Decoy effectAdding one “decoy” option that nobody would choose changes which of the other options people pick.
  16. Framing effectChanging only the way something is phrased, its frame, changes people’s decisions about it.
  17. Linda problemA problem showing how people judge “a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement” as more likely than “a bank teller.”
  18. Hot-hand fallacyThe long-held view that believing someone on a streak is “hot” and more likely to succeed next is an illusion. And the story of how that consensus got overturned.
  19. Monte Carlo fallacyThe mistaken belief that if a random outcome keeps repeating, the opposite outcome becomes due. Also called the gambler’s fallacy.
  20. Stroop effectWhen the word “red” is printed in blue ink, it takes longer to name the ink color.
  21. Cocktail party effectEven in a noisy place, you can’t help picking out your own name or a topic that interests you.
  22. Change blindnessFailing to notice even large changes in the scene right in front of you.
  23. The invisible gorillaAn experiment showing that when we’re focused on something, we can miss even something as absurd as a gorilla walking past.
  24. The jam studyAn experiment often cited as showing that too many choices make it harder for people to choose.
  25. Peak-end ruleWe judge past experiences largely by their most intense moment, the peak, and by how they ended.
  26. Proteus effectThe way the look of the avatar you use in a game or virtual world changes how you yourself behave.
  27. Fundamental attribution errorThe tendency to explain other people’s behavior by their character or ability rather than by their situation.
  28. Hyperbolic discountingOur habit of being patient about the distant future but impatient when a reward is right in front of us.
  29. Ulysses contractAn arrangement that binds your own future behavior now, anticipating that your future self will give in to temptation.
  30. False consensus effectThe tendency to assume your own opinions and preferences are shared by more people than they really are.
  31. SatisficingChoosing the first option that meets a “good enough” standard rather than searching on and on for the best.
  32. Rosenhan experimentA famous 1973 study testing what would happen if healthy people went to psychiatric hospitals saying they heard voices.
  33. Identifiable victim effectOne victim with a name and a face moves people far more than many victims counted in statistics.
  34. Psychological reactanceThe urge to push back and do the opposite when your freedom is restricted or someone pushes something on you too hard.
  35. Pollyanna syndromeExcessive optimism that looks so hard for the bright side that it ignores real problems.
  36. Müller-Lyer illusionTwo lines of equal length look longer with outward-pointing arrowheads at the ends and shorter with inward-pointing ones.
  37. Checker shadow illusionIn a picture of a checkerboard with a cylinder casting a shadow on it, two squares that look clearly different in color are actually exactly the same shade.
  38. ChronostasisWhen you glance at a clock, the second hand seems to freeze for longer than a second before it moves.
  39. Tanganyika laughter epidemicAn outbreak of laughing fits in 1962 in Tanganyika, now Tanzania, that began at a girls’ school and spread to surrounding villages.
  40. Clever HansA horse famous in early-20th-century Germany for doing arithmetic, which was actually reading unconscious cues from people.

Philosophy

  1. Chesterton’s fenceDon’t tear down a fence until you know why it was put up.
  2. Neurath’s boatOur knowledge is like a ship we must rebuild plank by plank while staying afloat at sea.
  3. Hanlon’s razorNever attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by incompetence.
  4. Münchhausen trilemmaTrace the reasons for any claim and you end in one of three places: an infinite regress, a circle, or an arbitrary stopping point.
  5. Experience machineA thought experiment: would you plug into a machine that gives you a lifetime of perfectly happy experiences?
  6. Hume’s guillotineYou cannot derive what ought to be from statements of what is.
  7. Gettier problemThe discovery that a belief can be true and well justified and still not count as knowledge.
  8. Brandolini’s lawThe effort needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude greater than the effort needed to produce it.
  9. Mary’s roomIf a scientist knew every physical fact about color but had never left a black-and-white room, would she learn something new the first time she saw red?
  10. Newcomb’s paradoxA predictor who almost never gets the future wrong has prepared two boxes. Do you take one box, or both?
  11. Buridan’s assA donkey standing exactly between two identical piles of hay that can’t choose either and starves.
  12. Motte-and-baileyMaking a bold claim, retreating to a modest, easy-to-defend one when challenged, then returning to the bold one once the danger has passed.
  13. SwampmanA thought experiment: if a being with exactly the same body and memories as a man killed by lightning arose from a swamp by chance, would it be him?
  14. Chinese roomA thought experiment: if someone who knows no Chinese could follow a rulebook to shuffle symbols and answer Chinese questions perfectly, would they understand Chinese?
  15. Sorites paradoxTake one grain of sand from a heap and it’s still a heap. Keep going, and is it still a heap when only one grain is left?
  16. Hempel’s ravensThe strange paradox that the hypothesis “all ravens are black” seems to be confirmed every time you find something non-black that isn’t a raven.
  17. Grue paradoxA paradox in which an odd color word, meaning “green if observed before a certain time, blue after,” throws our reasoning about the future into doubt.
  18. Hitchens’s razorThe principle that what is asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence.
  19. Newton’s flaming laser swordThe hard-line, science-minded view that any question that can’t be settled by experiment or observation isn’t worth debating.
  20. Sagan standardThe principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.
  21. Russell’s teapotThe analogy that nobody can disprove a claim that a tiny teapot orbits the sun between Earth and Mars, but that’s no reason to believe it.
  22. Pascal’s muggingThe paradox that however unlikely a story is, if the promised payoff is big enough, expected-value math says you should go along with it.
  23. Veil of ignoranceA thought experiment: design fair rules by imagining you don’t know what position in society you’ll be born into.
  24. Utility monsterA thought experiment: if a “monster” could feel vastly more happiness than anyone else, should everything be given to it to maximize total happiness?
  25. Repugnant conclusionThe problem that maximizing total happiness seems to imply that a huge population of lives barely worth living is better than a smaller, flourishing one.
  26. The owl of Minerva“The owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of the dusk.” Hegel’s line that we understand things only as they come to an end.
  27. Cunning of reasonHegel’s idea that even as individuals act on their desires and passions in history, the ends of “reason” are realized through them.
  28. SteelmanInstead of distorting an opponent’s argument into a weak version and knocking it down, rebuilding it in its strongest, most persuasive form before responding.
  29. WhataboutismDeflecting criticism by bringing up some other failing of the critic: “What about you?”
  30. Gish gallopA debating tactic of firing off so many claims and minor points in so little time that the opponent can’t possibly answer them all.
  31. No true ScotsmanProtecting a claim from a counterexample by redefining terms after the fact: “no real one would do that.”
  32. Moral luckThe problem that how we judge people’s actions depends on chance outcomes beyond their control.
  33. AkrasiaWeakness of will: knowing what you should do and doing something else.
  34. Twin EarthA thought experiment about meaning involving a planet identical to Earth except that the liquid called “water” there is a different substance.
  35. Philosophical zombieA hypothetical being that behaves exactly like a human from the outside but has no inner consciousness or experience at all.
  36. What is it like to be a bat?The question of whether knowing every physical fact about bats could ever tell you how the world feels to a bat. The answer argued: no.
  37. Paradox of toleranceThe paradox that if a tolerant society extends unlimited tolerance even to the intolerant, tolerance itself will eventually be destroyed.
  38. Heinz dilemmaA story posing a moral question: should a man who can’t afford the drug that could save his wife steal it?
  39. Wittgenstein’s ladderThe image that explanations used to reach understanding must be thrown away once you understand.
  40. Wittgenstein’s pokerA famous ten-minute argument at a Cambridge philosophy meeting in 1946, in which Wittgenstein reportedly brandished a fireplace poker at Karl Popper.
  41. Family resemblanceThe idea that things called by the same word don’t all share one common feature but are linked by overlapping partial similarities, like the faces in a family.
  42. Unintended consequencesWhen purposeful action produces results the actor never expected, sometimes the very opposite of what was intended.

Literature

  1. Involuntary memoryA memory that floods back on its own, triggered by a smell or a taste, without any effort to recall it.
  2. DefamiliarizationAn artistic technique that presents familiar things as if seen for the first time, so that we actually perceive them again.
  3. Objective correlativeExpressing an emotion not by naming it, but through a set of objects, situations or events that call it up.
  4. BovarysmeThe habit of imagining yourself as someone other, and more romantic, than you really are.
  5. Chekhov’s gunAnything introduced in a story must matter later on.
  6. Pathetic fallacyDescribing the sky, the rain, the trees, or anything else without a mind as if it had human feelings.
  7. Unreliable narratorA narrator who lies, misunderstands, or hides things, and whose account shouldn’t be taken at face value.
  8. In medias resStarting a story not at the beginning but in the middle of the action.
  9. HamartiaThe error of judgment or flaw that leads a tragic hero to ruin.
  10. Bechdel testA simple test: does a work have at least two women who talk to each other about something other than a man?
  11. Roman à clefA novel that portrays real people and events under changed names.
  12. BildungsromanA novel that follows its protagonist growing up as a person through experience. A coming-of-age novel.
  13. Red herringA false clue planted, in mysteries and elsewhere, to draw attention away from the real one.
  14. Negative capabilityThe ability to stay with uncertainty, where no answer comes quickly, without anxiously grasping for a conclusion.
  15. Gothic novelA strand of fiction set in old castles and ruins, full of the eerie, the terrifying, and the supernatural.
  16. PicaresqueA story in which a lowborn hero wanders from place to place, surviving on cunning. The rogue’s novel.
  17. The Library of BabelAn endless imaginary library holding a book for every possible combination of letters.
  18. Round characterA character too complex to sum up in a single trait, with unexpected sides that surprise the reader. The opposite is a flat character.
  19. AnagnorisisThe moment a character in a story realizes a crucial truth they hadn’t known. Also translated as recognition or discovery.
  20. MimesisArt’s imitation or representation of reality, and the way it goes about it.
  21. The death of the authorThe idea that a work’s meaning isn’t fixed by the author’s intentions but is created by readers as they read.
  22. IntertextualityThe idea that every text is built from quotations and echoes of countless texts written before it.
  23. The anxiety of influenceThe idea that later poets struggle to escape the influence of great predecessors in order to find a voice of their own.
  24. Magic realismA style that weaves magical, impossible events into realistic everyday description as if they were perfectly ordinary.
  25. Stream of consciousnessA technique that transcribes the thoughts, feelings, and associations flowing through a character’s mind as one unbroken current.
  26. EkphrasisVivid verbal description of a painting, sculpture, or other work of art, and the literary form that does it.
  27. BloomsdayJune 16, celebrated each year in Dublin and elsewhere in honor of James Joyce’s novel Ulysses.
  28. BowdlerizeTo gut a work by cutting or rewriting anything considered immoral or vulgar.
  29. Iceberg theoryA way of writing that deliberately leaves most of the story beneath the surface, so readers feel the weight of the whole from the small part that’s shown.
  30. The dog that didn’t barkThe idea that something failing to happen, when it should have, can be a crucial clue in itself.
  31. Knox’s DecalogueTen rules that detective fiction writers were supposed to follow.
  32. DoublethinkThe ability to believe two contradictory ideas at once without noticing the contradiction.
  33. Catch-22A no-win situation created by contradictory rules.
  34. KafkaesqueThe nightmarish feeling of being caught up in an absurd situation you can’t understand, helpless inside a vast, faceless system.
  35. The Philosophy of CompositionEdgar Allan Poe’s essay arguing that poems and stories aren’t born from inspiration but are built logically, working backward from the effect.
  36. The hero’s journeyThe story pattern said to appear in myths and tales worldwide: the hero leaves ordinary life, passes through trials, and returns transformed.
  37. The Scottish playThe name actors use for Macbeth, born of the superstition that saying its title inside a theater brings disaster.
  38. Narrative trickIn mystery fiction and elsewhere, a device that uses the way the text itself is written to mislead the reader.
  39. FlâneurSomeone who wanders city streets without purpose, enjoying watching the people and the life of the city.
  40. EpiphanyA moment when a small everyday event suddenly reveals the essence of something.

Art & Film

  1. PentimentoA trace of an earlier drawing or shape showing through a painting, where the artist changed their mind.
  2. Trompe-l’œilPainting so realistic and three-dimensional that it fools the eye into thinking it is real.
  3. Horror vacuiAn aversion to empty space that drives artists to fill every corner with decoration or pattern.
  4. Diabolus in musica“The devil in music”: the nickname for the unsettling tritone, an interval three whole steps wide, such as F to B.
  5. VanitasA genre of still life that shows the fleetingness of life through skulls, wilting flowers, guttering candles, and the like.
  6. MacGuffinThe thing the characters chase desperately, though what exactly it is barely matters to the story.
  7. Stendhal syndromeBeing so overwhelmed by great art that you feel dizzy or your heart races.
  8. Kuleshov effectThe same shot of an actor’s blank face seems to show different emotions depending on the shots placed before and after it.
  9. Wilhelm screamA stock sound effect of a man screaming that has been reused in hundreds of films and TV shows.
  10. Alan SmitheeA fictitious Hollywood director’s name used when a real director, unhappy with a film, didn’t want their name on it.
  11. Jump the sharkThe moment a long-running TV show resorts to an outlandish stunt for attention and clearly starts to decline.
  12. Women in RefrigeratorsThe trope in which a female character is harmed or killed just to motivate a male hero.
  13. Mary SueA too-perfect character who can do anything and is loved by everyone, as if mirroring the author’s wishes directly.
  14. RedshirtA nameless extra who appears in a story only to show the danger facing the main characters, and is quickly killed off.
  15. Grand GuignolA Paris theater from the late 19th to the mid-20th century famous for gory horror plays, and now a byword for lurid horror.
  16. Dolly zoomA shot in which the camera moves away from the subject while zooming in. The subject stays the same size while the background stretches or shrinks, creating a dizzying feeling.
  17. Diegetic soundSound in a film that the characters can hear too, as distinct from music played only for the audience from outside the story.
  18. Hollywood accountingStudio bookkeeping that makes a hit movie look like it lost money.
  19. Magic hourThe short window just after sunrise or just before sunset when light turns soft and warm. Photographers and filmmakers love it.
  20. SfumatoA painting technique that blurs the boundaries between colors like smoke, with no outlines, so tones shift softly into one another.
  21. AnamorphosisA technique of painting an image that looks distorted head-on and appears correctly only from a particular angle or in a mirror.
  22. International Klein BlueThe deep, vivid ultramarine created by the French artist Yves Klein.
  23. VantablackA material known as one of the blackest substances in the world, absorbing nearly all the light that hits it, and the feud among artists over who could use it.
  24. Prussian blueA synthetic blue pigment discovered by accident in early-18th-century Berlin. In Japan it was called bero-ai, Berlin indigo, and it transformed the blues of ukiyo-e prints.
  25. Mummy brownA brown paint made from ground-up Egyptian mummies and actually used until the 19th century.
  26. Scheele’s greenA vivid green pigment created in the 18th century that contained deadly arsenic.
  27. ReadymadePresenting a mass-produced object, as is or barely altered, as a work of art.
  28. AuraThe particular weight and glow a work of art has because it exists only once, here and now.
  29. KitschCheap, sentimental, imitation beauty that pretends to be real art.
  30. Theft of the Mona LisaThe 1911 theft of the Mona Lisa from the Louvre in Paris. It’s said to be what made it the most famous painting in the world.
  31. The van Meegeren affairThe case of the Dutch painter Han van Meegeren, whose forged Vermeers fooled experts and even a top Nazi.
  32. Art brutArt made by people without formal art training, from an inner impulse, unconcerned with critics or fashion.
  33. JaponismeThe craze for ukiyo-e prints and other Japanese art in late-19th-century Europe, and its influence on many painters.

Religion & Myth

  1. AcediaA spiritual listlessness. An old monastic word for the state where nothing seems worth the effort and the soul quietly dries out.
  2. MappōThe Buddhist idea that after the Buddha’s death, his teaching gradually decays until no one can reach enlightenment anymore.
  3. DemiurgeA god as craftsman who shaped the world. In Gnostic belief, a lesser and flawed maker of an imperfect world, distinct from the true God.
  4. EuhemerismThe theory that the gods of myth were originally real kings or heroes, worshipped as gods after they died.
  5. Scylla and CharybdisBeing caught between two dangers, where steering away from one brings you closer to the other.
  6. Apophatic theologyA way of approaching God by saying what God is not, layering negation upon negation, instead of saying what God is.
  7. Procrustean bedForcing things to fit a single standard, ignoring their particular circumstances.
  8. Cargo cultCopying the outward form of something without understanding what actually makes it work. Originally the name for religious movements in Melanesia.
  9. Mary Magdalene the penitent prostituteThe long-held image of Mary Magdalene, a follower of Jesus, as a repentant prostitute, which appears nowhere in the Bible.
  10. How many angels can dance on the head of a pin?A phrase used to mock medieval theologians for supposedly debating useless questions in deadly earnest.
  11. LilithIn Jewish tradition, Adam’s first wife, created before Eve.
  12. GolemIn Jewish folklore, an artificial man shaped from earth or clay and brought to life by the power of words.
  13. KalpaAn unimaginably long unit of time used in Buddhism and other religions of India.
  14. Indra’s netAn image of a net stretched across the universe, with a jewel at every knot, each jewel reflecting all the others.
  15. Parable of the poisoned arrowThe Buddha’s parable warning against getting so caught up in unanswerable questions that you neglect what needs doing now.
  16. Parable of the raftThe Buddha’s parable: teachings are like a raft for crossing a river, not something to carry on your back once you’re across.
  17. The sound of one handA question given in Zen training: two hands clapped together make a sound. What is the sound of one hand?
  18. No meritA Zen saying: good deeds done in hope of reward carry no true merit.
  19. Goryō beliefThe Japanese belief that the spirits of those who died unjustly can bring curses, and that enshrining them as gods will appease them.

Science

  1. Lazarus taxonA living thing that was thought to be extinct, then turns up alive after a long gap in the record.
  2. Olbers’ paradoxIf the universe were infinite and evenly filled with stars, the night sky should be as bright as day. So why is it dark?
  3. CarcinizationCrustaceans that are not crabs keep evolving into crab-shaped creatures, independently, over and over.

  6. Keystone speciesA species that, even in small numbers, holds the structure of an entire ecosystem together.
  7. Roche limitThe distance at which a moon that gets too close to a planet is torn apart by tidal forces.
  8. Pale Blue DotThe 1990 photograph of Earth taken by Voyager 1 from about 6 billion kilometers (3.7 billion miles) away, showing our planet as a tiny speck of light.

  10. Red Queen hypothesisBecause enemies and rivals keep evolving too, a species has to keep evolving just to hold its ground.
  11. Great FilterThe hypothesis that somewhere between the origin of life and a civilization that spreads through space lies a barrier that almost nothing gets past.
  12. Boltzmann brainA brain, complete with memories, that pops into existence by chance from a random thermal fluctuation. A strange thought experiment in cosmology.
  13. Mpemba effectThe claim that, under some conditions, warm water can freeze faster than cold water.
  14. Maxwell’s demonAn imaginary being from a thought experiment: by sorting gas molecules one at a time and opening and closing a little door, could it create a temperature difference without using energy?
  15. Piltdown ManA faked fossil “discovered” in England in 1912 and accepted as a human ancestor for more than 40 years.
  16. N-raysA form of radiation announced by a French physicist in 1903 and “observed” by many researchers, which didn’t actually exist.
  17. PolywaterA supposed polymerized form of water with strange properties that excited researchers worldwide in the 1960s, and turned out to be contaminated water.
  18. Cold fusionNuclear fusion supposedly achieved in a tabletop device near room temperature, announced in 1989 to a worldwide sensation.
  19. Pathological scienceScience in which researchers fool themselves into believing in a phenomenon that doesn’t exist.
  20. Replication crisisThe problem that many published research findings can’t be reproduced when other researchers repeat the same methods.
  21. PhlogistonAn imaginary substance that 18th-century chemists believed escaped from things as they burned, the stuff of combustion itself.
  22. Luminiferous aetherAn invisible substance once thought to fill space so that light had something to travel through.
  23. Prince Rupert’s dropA tadpole-shaped glass bead made by dripping molten glass into cold water. Its head survives a hammer blow, but snap the tip of its tail and the whole thing shatters instantly.
  24. Leidenfrost effectDrop water onto a red-hot pan and, instead of boiling away at once, the drops bead up and skitter around.
  25. Brazil nut effectShake a container of mixed-size particles and the big ones rise to the top.
  26. Cherenkov radiationThe blue glow given off when a charged particle races through a material like water faster than light travels in that material.
  27. Radium GirlsWomen in 1920s America whose bodies were ravaged by radiation from painting glowing watch dials, and the lawsuit they brought.
  28. Canary in a coal mineSomething that warns of approaching danger earlier than anything else.
  29. Cavendish experimentThe 1798 experiment in which the English scientist Henry Cavendish used a torsion balance to measure the tiny gravitational pull between objects.
  30. Heat death of the universeA distant future in which the universe reaches a uniform temperature, the flow of energy stops, and nothing ever changes again.
  31. Magdeburg hemispheresA 17th-century experiment: two metal hemispheres were fitted together and the air pumped out, and teams of horses couldn’t pull them apart.
  32. Galileo’s Leaning Tower experimentThe famous story that Galileo dropped balls of different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to show they hit the ground together. Whether he actually did it is doubtful.
  33. Haber–Bosch processA method of making ammonia from nitrogen in the air. It made mass production of chemical fertilizer possible and sustains much of the world’s population.
  34. Midgley’s inventionsLeaded gasoline and CFCs, two inventions that later did enormous harm to the planet, both created by the American engineer Thomas Midgley.
  35. Keeling CurveThe graph of atmospheric carbon dioxide measured continuously on Hawaii’s Mauna Loa since 1958, zigzagging upward year after year.
  36. Ultraviolet catastropheThe contradiction that 19th-century physics predicted a hot object should radiate infinite energy. Resolving it gave birth to quantum theory.
  37. YtterbyA small village in Sweden whose mine yielded a mineral that gave four chemical elements their names.
  38. NipponiumAn element announced by a Japanese chemist in 1908 and later withdrawn. He very likely had found a different new element.
  39. Eka-siliconThe undiscovered element Mendeleev predicted for a gap in his periodic table, naming it and forecasting its properties. It turned out to be germanium.
  40. Arago spotShine light on a round obstacle and a bright spot appears right in the center of its shadow.
  41. Brownian motionThe ceaseless, random jiggling of tiny particles suspended in water. It became evidence that atoms and molecules really exist.

Math & Computing

  1. Benford’s lawIn many real-world sets of numbers, about 30 percent start with the digit 1, and fewer than 5 percent start with 9.
  2. Rubber duck debuggingExplaining your code line by line to a rubber duck until you spot the bug yourself.

  15. Taxicab numberThe smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in a given number of ways. The smallest that works in two ways is 1729.
  16. Collatz conjectureThe unsolved conjecture that, starting from any positive whole number, if you halve it when it’s even and triple it and add one when it’s odd, you will always end up at 1.
  17. Kaprekar’s constant6174, the number that nearly every four-digit number reaches if you repeatedly arrange its digits into the largest and smallest numbers and subtract.
  18. Ham sandwich theoremHowever two slices of bread and a slice of ham are shaped and wherever they sit, a single straight cut can split all three exactly in half.

  21. GoogolTen to the 100th power: a 1 followed by 100 zeros.

  26. Penrose tilingA strange way of covering a plane with just two shapes of tile, leaving no gaps, in which the pattern never repeats.

  28. Nicolas BourbakiA fictional mathematician who had a huge influence on 20th-century mathematics. In reality, the pen name of a group of young French mathematicians.
  29. Indiana Pi BillAn 1897 bill in the Indiana legislature that came close to effectively writing an incorrect value of pi into law.
  30. Buffon’s needleDrop needles onto a floor ruled with evenly spaced parallel lines, work out the chance a needle crosses a line, and pi appears.

  36. Anscombe’s quartetFour sets of data with nearly identical averages, correlations, and other statistics that look completely different when plotted.

  38. Streetlight effectThe tendency to search where it’s easiest to look rather than where the answer is likely to be.
  39. McNamara fallacyValuing only what can be measured and treating what can’t be measured as unimportant, or as if it doesn’t exist.
  40. Bayesian search theoryA way of searching for something lost by updating your estimate of where it’s likely to be after each search, then deciding where to look next.

  47. Dining philosophers problemFive philosophers at a round table each need the two forks they share with their neighbors to eat, and they can end up all stuck.
  48. Knuth reward checkThe $2.56 check the computer scientist Donald Knuth sends to the first person to find each error in his books.
  49. Garbage in, garbage outThe principle that if you feed bad data into even the best system, you’ll only get bad results.

  51. CAPTCHAA test on websites, like reading warped letters or picking out images, that tells humans and programs apart.

  56. Hello, world!The tiny program that just prints “Hello, world” on the screen, often the first thing written when learning to program.

  58. SpamUnwanted messages, usually ads, sent out in bulk to people who never asked for them.
  59. Fencepost errorConfusing the number of intervals with the number of dividers and counting one too many or one too few.

Architecture & Cities

  33. LimeyA nickname, mainly American, for British sailors and British people, from the lime juice drunk aboard ship to prevent scurvy.

  20. Sunset clauseA provision that sets an expiration date for a law or program, so that it lapses automatically unless it’s actively renewed.
  21. LogrollingLawmakers trading votes by promising to support each other’s bills.
  22. Habeas corpusThe right of someone held unlawfully to go to court, make the authorities justify the detention, and be freed if they can’t.
  23. Ignorance of the law is no excuseThe ancient legal principle that not knowing a law doesn’t excuse breaking it.
  24. The Paisley snailThe case of a woman in Paisley, Scotland, who said a snail came out of her drink bottle in 1928. It became the starting point of the modern law of negligence.
  25. The McDonald’s coffee caseThe 1990s American lawsuit by a woman who suffered severe burns from spilled McDonald’s coffee. For years it was a punch line about a lawsuit-crazy society.
  26. Daylight saving timeSetting clocks ahead by about an hour in summer to make better use of daylight.
  27. Ox-walk tacticA form of resistance in Japan’s parliament in which lawmakers walk to the ballot box deliberately slowly, like oxen, to stall a vote.
  28. Tamamushi-iroJapanese for “jewel-beetle color”: deliberately ambiguous wording or a settlement that each side can interpret its own way.
  29. NemawashiTalking things through with the people involved and securing their agreement before a formal decision is made. Originally a gardening term.
  30. OstracismA practice in ancient Athens in which citizens voted by scratching names on pottery shards to exile someone who might become a tyrant for ten years.
  31. SortitionChoosing representatives from among the citizens by lottery instead of election, to join in political deliberation and decision-making.
  32. An eye for an eyeThe ancient legal principle that whoever injures another’s eye shall have their own eye injured. Often taken as a call for revenge, it originally aimed to keep retaliation from going too far.

Economics & Money

  1. BezzleThe wealth that exists only between the moment of a fraud and its discovery, when the victim and the thief both feel richer.
  2. Rai stones of YapStone money from the Micronesian island of Yap. The biggest pieces are taller than a person.
  3. Keynesian beauty contestKeynes’s analogy for investing: the game is not picking what you think is best, but what you think everyone else will think is best.
  4. Parable of the broken windowA short story exposing the mistake of thinking that a broken window is good for the economy because it gives the glazier work.
  5. Gresham’s law“Bad money drives out good.” When debased and full-value coins circulate at the same face value, people hoard the good ones and spend the bad.
  6. Giffen goodA rare kind of product that people buy more of when its price goes up.
  7. Minsky momentThe moment when debt-fueled speculation built up during good times suddenly collapses and asset prices crash.
  8. Money illusionFeeling richer or poorer because of the number on your paycheck, without accounting for how prices have changed.
  9. Paradox of valueWater is essential to life yet cheap, while diamonds are unnecessary yet expensive. The classic economic puzzle is how to explain that gap.
  10. Lipstick effectThe theory that in a downturn, people skip big luxuries and buy affordable little indulgences, like lipstick, instead.
  11. Cantillon effectThe idea that newly created money doesn’t spread evenly through an economy, and those who receive it first benefit most.
  12. Big Mac indexA rough gauge of whether currencies are overvalued or undervalued, based on what a Big Mac costs in different countries.
  13. Animal spiritsThe urge and optimism, rather than careful calculation, that push people to invest or start a venture.
  14. Laffer curveA curve illustrating the idea that if tax rates rise too high, people work and invest less, and tax revenue actually falls.
  15. St. Petersburg paradoxA gamble whose expected payout is mathematically infinite, yet nobody would pay much to play it.
  16. Tulip maniaThe episode in 17th-century Holland when prices for rare tulip bulbs soared to absurd heights before suddenly collapsing in 1637. Often called the first financial bubble.
  17. Winner’s curseThe phenomenon that the winner of an auction has often paid more than the thing is really worth.
  18. Dollar auctionA game in which a dollar bill is auctioned to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid.
  19. Market for lemonsThe theory that in a market where only sellers know the quality of what they’re selling, bad goods crowd out the good ones.
  20. 100 trillion dollar noteA banknote with a face value of 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollars, issued in 2009 during the African nation’s record-breaking hyperinflation.
  21. Helicopter moneyThe idea of a central bank creating new money and handing it straight to the public, as if scattering it from a helicopter.
  22. Trickle-down economicsThe idea that if the wealthy and big corporations get richer first, their wealth will drip down and eventually benefit everyone.
  23. The deadweight loss of ChristmasThe economic observation that gifts are worth less to their recipients than the giver paid, so the difference is value destroyed.
  24. Hotelling’s lawCompeting shops and products, fighting over customers, end up surprisingly similar in location and content.
  25. The pin factoryAdam Smith’s illustration, at the start of The Wealth of Nations, of how dividing work into specialized tasks multiplies output.
  26. The candlemakers’ petitionA satire in which candlemakers petition parliament for a law to shutter all windows, protecting them from unfair “foreign competition” from the sun.
  27. The Landlord’s GameAn early-20th-century American board game that became the prototype of Monopoly. It was designed to show the evils of land monopoly.
  28. Ponzi schemeA fraud that makes money look like it’s growing by paying earlier investors “returns” out of money from later ones, without investing anything.
  29. JiaoziOne of the world’s oldest paper currencies, born in China under the Northern Song dynasty in the 11th century.
  30. Dōjima Rice ExchangeA rice exchange in Edo-period Osaka, officially recognized by the shogunate in 1730, considered the world’s first organized futures market.
  31. Three months’ salaryThe rule of thumb, long accepted in Japan, that an engagement ring should cost three months’ salary. It actually spread from a diamond company’s advertising.
  32. There’s no such thing as a free lunchThe saying that whatever you get, you’re paying for it somehow. Known as an expression of a basic idea in economics.
  33. Baumol’s cost diseaseWhile some industries grow more efficient through mechanization, work that can’t easily be made more efficient gets relatively more expensive over time.

Music

  1. EarwormWhen a fragment of a song plays over and over in your head on its own.
  2. Picardy thirdEnding a piece in a minor key on a bright major chord.
  3. Tristan chordThe ambiguous, unstable chord at the opening of Wagner’s opera Tristan und Isolde that never seems to settle anywhere.
  4. Mozart effectThe claim that listening to Mozart makes you smarter. The actual research found something far more modest.
  5. Loudness warThe competition to make recorded music ever louder to stand out, which drained it of its dynamics.
  6. Shepard toneAn auditory illusion of a tone that seems to rise (or fall) forever.
  7. Devil’s Trill SonataA violin sonata by the Italian composer Giuseppe Tartini, said to be based on music the devil played in his dream.
  8. Pythagorean commaThe tiny gap between stacking twelve pure fifths and stacking seven octaves, which should land on the same note but don’t quite.
  9. Music of the spheresThe ancient idea that the motions of the sun, moon, and planets sound beautiful harmonies too subtle for the ear to hear.
  10. ThereminAn electronic instrument played without touching it, just by moving the hands in the air.
  11. Brown M&M’sThe famous story of a rock band writing “no brown M&M’s in the dressing room” into its concert contracts. It wasn’t diva behavior but a built-in test.
  12. The Well-Tempered ClavierBach’s collection of preludes and fugues in every major and minor key. “Well-tempered,” though, doesn’t necessarily mean today’s equal temperament.
  13. Guidonian handA medieval method of teaching singing by assigning note names to the joints of the hand, named after the monk credited with inventing do-re-mi.
  14. Allegri’s MiserereA piece of church music kept secret by the Vatican, which the 14-year-old Mozart is said to have written down from memory after a single hearing.
  15. Bach revivalThe 19th-century rediscovery of Bach’s music, half forgotten after his death, sparked by the 20-year-old Felix Mendelssohn’s performance of the St Matthew Passion.

Food

  1. Cans and can openersA quirk of food history: nearly half a century passed between the invention of canned food and the arrival of the can opener.
  2. Maillard reactionThe chemical reaction between sugars and amino acids that gives food a savory aroma and a brown crust when it’s roasted, grilled, or fried.
  3. Scoville scaleA measure of chili pepper heat, expressed as how many times an extract has to be diluted before the heat can no longer be tasted.
  4. Miracle fruitA red berry native to West Africa that, for a while after you eat it, makes sour foods taste sweet.
  5. Fortune cookieThe crisp cookie with a fortune inside served after meals at Chinese restaurants in America. Its roots, it turns out, are probably Japanese.
  6. Doyō no Ushi no HiThe Day of the Ox in the midsummer doyō period of the old Japanese calendar. The custom of eating grilled eel on this day spread in the Edo period.
  7. Peach MelbaA dessert of peaches and vanilla ice cream topped with raspberry sauce, created in the late 19th century for an opera singer.
  8. ParmentierThe 18th-century French pharmacist who promoted the potato, then seen as animal feed, as food for people. His name lives on in potato dishes.
  9. Judgment of ParisA 1976 blind wine tasting in Paris in which little-known California wines beat prestigious French ones.
  10. The croissant legendThe famous tale that the croissant was invented to celebrate Vienna’s defeat of the Ottoman siege in 1683, shaped like the crescent on the enemy’s flags.
  11. Run IslandA tiny island in Indonesia’s Banda Islands, famous for being effectively swapped for Manhattan in the 17th century over nutmeg.
  12. KetchupThe name of the tomato condiment, which originally meant an Asian fish sauce with no tomatoes in it at all.
  13. Kaldi’s goatsThe legend of Kaldi, an Ethiopian goatherd, and the discovery of coffee.
  14. Hand pollination of vanillaPollinating vanilla flowers by hand, one at a time, a method devised in 1841 by a 12-year-old boy on an island in the Indian Ocean.
  15. NarezushiThe oldest form of sushi: fish packed in salt and rice and fermented over a long time.
  16. Kombu RoadThe trade route along which kelp from Hokkaido traveled in the Edo period by ship to Osaka, then on to Satsuma, the Ryukyu Kingdom, and China.
  17. TempuraThe Japanese dish of fish and vegetables coated in batter and deep-fried. Its name most likely comes from Portuguese.
  18. Sonic chipAn experiment showing that amplifying the crunch of potato chips makes them taste fresher and better.
  19. French paradoxThe apparent contradiction that the French eat a diet rich in butter, meat, and fat yet have relatively low rates of death from heart disease.
  20. BetsubaraJapanese for “a separate stomach”: being too full to eat another bite yet still having room for dessert. Science can explain some of it.

Geography & Earth

  1. Coastline paradoxThe finer the ruler you use to measure a coastline, the longer it gets, so it never settles on a single length.
  2. Point NemoThe spot in the South Pacific farthest from any land. The nearest island is about 2,700 kilometers (1,670 miles) away.
  3. Bir TawilA patch of about 2,000 square kilometers (800 square miles) on the Egypt–Sudan border that neither country claims.
  4. BaarleA town where the border between Belgium and the Netherlands is tangled like a mosaic. Sometimes it runs right through a house.
  5. International Date LineThe north–south line across the Pacific where the date changes. Cross it and you jump forward or back a day.
  6. Mercator projectionA way of drawing world maps devised in 1569. It keeps angles true, which is handy for navigation, but the farther land is from the equator, the bigger it’s drawn.
  7. ChibanianThe name, given in 2020, of a stage of Earth’s history from about 774,000 to about 129,000 years ago, after rock layers in Chiba, Japan.
  8. Wallace LineA boundary in animal distribution running between the Indonesian islands of Bali and Lombok and beyond. Across it, Asian fauna gives way abruptly to Australian fauna.
  9. Brocken spectreOn a mountain with the sun at your back, your shadow cast onto mist or cloud appears surrounded by a rainbow-like ring of light.
  10. Fata MorganaA kind of mirage, often seen over the sea, in which distant scenery stretches upward or floats, as if castles or cities hung in the air.
  11. Green flashA brief green flash of light seen at the very moment the sun sets below, or rises above, the horizon.
  12. Door to HellA huge crater in the desert of Turkmenistan in Central Asia that is said to have been burning for more than half a century.
  13. 1883 eruption of KrakatoaThe cataclysmic 1883 eruption of the Krakatoa volcano in Indonesia, whose explosion is said to be the loudest sound in recorded history.
  14. Genbudō CaveA cave in Toyooka, Hyōgo Prefecture, Japan, lined with hexagonal stone columns. The Japanese word for basalt, genbugan, comes from its name.
  15. GeoidEarth’s “true shape,” imagined as the sea surface extended under the continents. It’s lumpy, like a potato.
  16. ChimborazoA mountain in Ecuador. It’s about 2,600 meters lower than Everest, but its summit is the point on Earth farthest from the planet’s center.
  17. Undetermined prefectural boundariesPlaces in Japan where the border between prefectures has never been settled. The summit area of Mount Fuji is one of them.
  18. Kitayama VillageJapan’s only enclave village: part of Wakayama Prefecture, yet surrounded by Mie and Nara and touching no other Wakayama municipality.
  19. Lake ConstanceA lake bordered by Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, where the three countries have never agreed where the borders on the water lie.
  20. Paris meridianThe line of longitude through the Paris Observatory. For a long time France used it, not Greenwich, as its reference for longitude.

Living Things

  1. Immortal jellyfishA tiny jellyfish that, after reaching maturity, can turn back into its juvenile polyp stage when it ages or is injured, and start life over.
  2. TardigradeA tiny animal under a millimeter long that lumbers along on eight legs. When it dries out, it curls into a barrel-shaped dormant state that survives extreme conditions.
  3. Naked mole-ratA hairless rodent that lives underground in East Africa in colonies built around a queen, much like ants or bees.
  4. Batesian mimicryWhen a harmless creature evolves to look like a poisonous one and is attacked less as a result.
  5. Zombie antAnts infected by certain fungi climb plants as if under remote control, clamp their jaws onto a leaf, and die there.
  6. The eel questionWhere and how eels are born, a mystery that lasted more than 2,000 years.
  7. Short-tailed albatrossA large seabird with a wingspan of more than two meters. Its Japanese name, ahōdori, means “fool bird,” because it showed no fear of people and was easy to catch on land.
  8. Waggle danceA figure-eight dance honeybees perform to tell their hivemates the direction and distance of flowers.
  9. Bird-of-paradiseBirds with dazzling ornamental plumes found in New Guinea and nearby. Europeans once believed they were legless birds of heaven.
  10. Dwarf maleA male far smaller than the females of its species. In some deep-sea anglerfish, the male fuses with the female’s body.
  11. Recurrent laryngeal nerveA nerve from the brain that works the muscles of the larynx. For some reason it first runs down into the chest, loops under a blood vessel near the heart, and then climbs back up to the throat.
  12. The panda’s thumbThe thumb-like knob on a giant panda’s front paw, which isn’t a real thumb at all but an enlarged wrist bone.
  13. JumboA word meaning “huge,” which spread from the name of an African elephant who became a worldwide celebrity in the 19th century.
  14. Passenger pigeonA pigeon that once numbered in the billions in North America and was driven extinct by human hunting in the early 20th century.
  15. Siebold’s giant salamanderA Japanese giant salamander that Philipp Franz von Siebold sent to the Netherlands in the Edo period, which lived there for more than 50 years.
  16. Lazy antsResearch showing that even in ant colonies, the very symbol of hard work, a certain share of ants barely work, and their presence helps the colony last.
  17. Slime moldA single-celled organism that creeps around with an amoeba-like body, spreading out in search of food. Without a brain, it can solve mazes and build efficient networks.
  18. Temperature-dependent sex determinationIn sea turtles, crocodiles, and some other reptiles, the temperature of the sand or soil around the eggs determines whether the hatchlings are male or female.
  19. Whale songThe long, intricately structured songs sung by male humpback whales in the breeding season.
  20. Leafcutter antAnts of Central and South America that cut leaves, carry them home, and use them to grow the fungus they eat.
  21. Food sharing in vampire batsVampire bats, which live on blood, regurgitate some of their meal to feed companions who came back hungry that night.
  22. PlatypusAn Australian mammal with a bill and webbed feet that lays eggs yet feeds its young on milk.

Space

  1. Wow! signalA strong, sharp radio signal from space picked up by an American radio telescope in 1977. People still wonder whether it came from an alien civilization.
  2. Voyager Golden RecordA record of sounds and images from Earth carried aboard the Voyager 1 and 2 probes, launched in 1977. A letter to anyone out there we might someday meet.
  3. Dyson sphereA hypothetical megastructure that an advanced civilization might build around a star to capture all of its energy.
  4. The demotion of PlutoThe 2006 decision by the International Astronomical Union to define “planet” and reclassify Pluto as a dwarf planet.
  5. VulcanA phantom planet, predicted in the 19th century to orbit closer to the sun than Mercury, that many people searched for.
  6. ʻOumuamuaThe first object confirmed to have come from outside the solar system, discovered in 2017.
  7. RecombinationAbout 380,000 years after the universe began, light could finally travel in straight lines and the universe became transparent.
  8. Titius–Bode lawAn 18th-century rule of thumb that the planets’ distances from the sun roughly follow a simple number sequence.
  9. Martian canalsA network of lines that astronomers in the late 19th century believed they saw on the surface of Mars, sparking talk of canals built by Martians.
  10. EarthshineThe faint glow on the dark part of the moon during a thin crescent, caused by sunlight reflected off Earth.
  11. Moon illusionThe optical illusion that makes the moon look much bigger near the horizon than high in the sky.
  12. Overview effectThe profound sense of Earth’s unity, without borders, and of its fragility that astronauts experience when they see it from space.
  13. Carrington EventThe 1859 solar storm, among the largest on record. Auroras were seen worldwide and telegraph systems went haywire.
  14. Maunder MinimumA stretch of about 70 years, from the late 17th to the early 18th century, when sunspots almost vanished.
  15. SpaghettificationWhat happens to an object falling toward a black hole: powerful tidal forces stretch it lengthwise and squeeze it sideways until it’s long and thin, like spaghetti.
  16. Little Green MenThe joking provisional name given to the first pulsar, a celestial object emitting perfectly regular radio pulses, discovered in 1967.
  17. Pioneer anomalyA 30-year mystery: the space probes Pioneer 10 and 11, launched in the 1970s, seemed to be slowing down ever so slightly more than calculations predicted.
  18. Harvard ComputersThe women who, from the late 19th century, did the calculations and classifications of star photographs at the Harvard College Observatory. Back then, “computers” meant people who computed.

Technology

  1. Mechanical TurkA chess-playing automaton that amazed 18th-century Europe. In reality, a skilled human chess player was hidden inside its cabinet.
  2. Mars Climate OrbiterAn American space probe lost in 1999 as it tried to enter orbit around Mars. The cause was mixed-up units.
  3. Normalization of devianceWhen small anomalies or rule-breaking that should never be allowed pass without trouble often enough, they quietly come to be accepted as normal.
  4. Phoebus cartelAn international cartel formed in 1924 by the world’s leading light bulb makers to limit bulb life to 1,000 hours.
  5. Rube Goldberg machineA contraption that does a simple task the hard way, through a deliberately elaborate chain reaction of rolling balls, falling dominoes, and swinging levers.
  6. QWERTYThe keyboard layout whose top-left letters run Q, W, E, R, T, Y. It’s inherited from 19th-century typewriters.
  7. International Prototype of the KilogramThe platinum-iridium cylinder that served as the world’s standard for one kilogram from 1889 to 2019.
  8. The longitude problemThe great challenge of the age of sail: there was no reliable way to know a ship’s east–west position at sea.
  9. Antikythera mechanismA geared astronomical calculator more than 2,000 years old, found in 1901 in a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera.
  10. Roman concreteThe concrete of ancient Rome, so durable that some of its buildings still stand nearly 2,000 years later.
  11. AeolipileA sphere spun by jets of steam, described by the engineer Hero of Alexandria around the first century. One of the earliest steam engines.
  12. BiomimicryCreating new technology and products by imitating the forms and mechanisms of living things.
  13. SkeuomorphismKeeping the shape or look of an old tool in a new tool or design, even though it no longer serves its original purpose.
  14. BugA defect in a computer program or machine. Literally, an insect.
  15. LudditesA movement of workers in early-19th-century England who went around smashing new machines. Today the word also means anyone who dislikes new technology.
  16. Iron RingAn iron or stainless steel ring Canadian engineers receive in a ceremony affirming their responsibilities, worn on the little finger of the working hand.
  17. Tea-serving dollAn Edo-period mechanical doll that walks a cup of tea over to a guest, stops when the cup is lifted, and turns around and walks back when it’s replaced.
  18. Yagi–Uda antennaAn antenna that efficiently picks up radio waves from a particular direction, developed in the 1920s by Hidetsugu Yagi and Shintarō Uda at Tōhoku Imperial University. It’s still used for TV antennas and more.
  19. QR codeA two-dimensional code of small black and white squares that stores information, developed in Japan in 1994.
