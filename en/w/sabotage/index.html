Sabotage

Language

Category: Language言 葉

In short

Deliberately slacking off or breaking machines or systems to obstruct someone’s activities.

The story

It comes from the French sabot, a wooden shoe. A popular story says it began with workers throwing their clogs into machines to stop them, but the likelier origin is a verb meaning to clatter along in clogs or to do a job clumsily. In the late 19th century it spread through the French labor movement as a word for a method of protest.

The lens

The word reached Japan around the 1910s and 1920s along with labor disputes, and eventually became the everyday verb saboru, to skip class or slack off, popular among students. Every time a Japanese student skips a lecture, a French clog is clattering somewhere.

Drop it casually

“Skipping today’s meeting is a protest in the proud tradition of the French wooden shoe. I’m just being faithful to the roots of sabotage.”
