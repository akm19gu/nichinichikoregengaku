Bouba/kiki effect

Language

Category: Language言 葉

In short

Show people a rounded shape and a spiky one and ask which is “bouba” and which is “kiki,” and nearly everyone calls the rounded one bouba.

The story

It began with an experiment the Gestalt psychologist Wolfgang Köhler described in a 1929 book, matching nonsense words to shapes (the pair “takete” and “maluma” from a later edition is the best known). In 2001 the neuroscientists V. S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard tried it with “bouba” and “kiki” and reported that over 90% of people, whether English or Tamil speakers, gave the same answer.

The lens

Linguistics generally holds that the link between a word’s sound and its meaning is arbitrary, and this gives that idea a gentle shake. It’s handy when thinking up names for products or characters, too.

Drop it casually

“This snack is rock hard but its name sounds so round. Feels wrong, bouba/kiki-wise.”
